After a year of baking a loaf every weekend, I have settled on a dough that sits at around seventy-five percent hydration. It is wet enough to give an open, glossy crumb, but still firm enough that I can shape it without the dough sticking to everything on the bench.
The biggest lesson was that the flour matters as much as the water. A strong bread flour can absorb far more water than an all-purpose flour, so a recipe that works perfectly with one can turn into soup with the other. When switching flours, I now hold back about a tenth of the water and add it slowly during mixing.
Temperature is the other variable people underestimate. In winter my kitchen is cold, and the bulk fermentation can take twice as long as it does in summer. Rather than following the clock, I watch for the dough to grow by roughly half and to show bubbles along the sides of the container.
Every loaf teaches you something, even the flat ones.
